# The Qualitative Factor in Software Testing

Software testing research has long focused on measurable outcomes — error rates, coverage metrics, and tool effectiveness. But behind every test suite is a human making decisions: what to test, how to test it, and how to interpret results. This study maps the qualitative research landscape in software testing to understand how the field has studied these human factors.

## What We Did

1. We conducted a systematic mapping study of qualitative research in software testing.
2. We identified and analyzed 102 primary studies published between 2003 and 2023.
3. We categorized studies by research strategy (e.g., case study, grounded theory), data collection method (e.g., interviews, observation), and data analysis method (e.g., thematic analysis, coding).
4. We assessed the quality of reporting and methodological rigor across the selected studies.

## What We Found

- **Case studies** and **grounded theory** are the most frequently used research strategies in qualitative software testing research.
- **Semi-structured interviews** are the dominant data collection method, used to understand how practitioners work and gather stakeholder perspectives.
- **Thematic analysis** is the most common approach to analyzing qualitative data.
- The most studied areas are **software testing processes and risks** and **test automation**.
- Underrepresented areas include **test oracles** and **machine learning-based testing** — both growing in practice but lacking qualitative investigation.
- Reporting quality and methodological rigor vary considerably across the body of literature.

## Why It Matters

Quantitative metrics alone cannot capture why developers make the testing decisions they do. Qualitative methods reveal the experiences, perceptions, and reasoning behind testing practices — knowledge that is essential for designing better tools and processes. This mapping study provides researchers and practitioners with a consolidated view of what has been studied, what methods work, and where significant gaps remain, helping direct future research toward the human side of software quality.
